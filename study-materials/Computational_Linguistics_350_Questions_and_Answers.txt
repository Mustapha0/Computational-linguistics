PART A. GENERAL ORIENTATION AND PREFACE (Questions 1–25)
================================================================================

1. What is computational linguistics?
Answer: It is the study of language using computational methods and the development
of computer systems that analyze, interpret, retrieve, translate, or generate language.

2. Why does computational linguistics combine linguistics and computer science?
Answer: Linguistics describes language patterns and meaning; computer science provides
formal representations, algorithms, data structures, and implementation methods.

3. Why is natural language difficult for computers to process?
Answer: It is ambiguous, context-sensitive, variable, productive, and shaped by grammar,
meaning, discourse, and social conventions.

4. What is the difference between a linguistic theory and a language-processing system?
Answer: A theory explains or describes linguistic patterns; a system implements
procedures for solving a defined task using representations and algorithms.

5. Why is formalization important in computational linguistics?
Answer: It makes assumptions explicit and turns observations into rules or procedures
that can be implemented, tested, compared, and revised.

6. What is a language corpus?
Answer: A corpus is a collection of language data assembled for a particular research
or application purpose.

7. Does a larger corpus automatically guarantee better results?
Answer: No. Data quality, representativeness, annotation, domain match, and duplication
also affect performance.

8. What is linguistic annotation?
Answer: It is the addition of labels or structures to language data, such as part-of-speech
tags, syntactic relations, entities, or semantic roles.

9. Why do annotation guidelines matter?
Answer: They help annotators apply labels consistently and make the resulting data
interpretable and useful for training or evaluation.

10. What is the difference between raw text and annotated text?
Answer: Raw text contains the original linguistic material; annotated text includes
additional labels or structural information.

11. What is meant by a language-processing pipeline?
Answer: It is a sequence of components, such as tokenization, parsing, and semantic
analysis, that transform input into a desired output.

12. What is error propagation?
Answer: It occurs when an incorrect result from an earlier component harms the output
of later components.

13. Why should a system be evaluated on data not used to build it?
Answer: Unseen evaluation data provides better evidence of whether the system generalizes
rather than merely memorizes or fits its development examples.

14. What is generalization?
Answer: Generalization is the ability to perform well on relevant cases beyond the data
used during development or training.

15. What is the difference between a prototype and a production system?
Answer: A prototype demonstrates an idea under limited conditions; a production system
must handle realistic inputs, failures, scale, and maintenance.

16. What is coverage in language technology?
Answer: Coverage is the range of words, constructions, inputs, or situations the system
can handle.

17. What is robustness?
Answer: Robustness is the ability to remain useful when input varies, contains noise,
or differs from the examples used during development.

18. Why can a fluent output still be incorrect?
Answer: Fluency concerns how natural the language sounds; factual accuracy and meaning
preservation are separate qualities.

19. Why is the application domain important?
Answer: Vocabulary, style, data patterns, and acceptable errors differ between domains
such as medicine, education, law, and casual conversation.

20. What is a false positive in language processing?
Answer: It is a case incorrectly identified as positive—for example, a correct word
wrongly flagged as misspelled.

21. What is a false negative?
Answer: It is a real case the system fails to identify, such as an actual spelling error
that the checker overlooks.

22. Why should a computational model state its limitations?
Answer: Users and researchers need to know where results may be unreliable and what
kinds of input the system was not designed to handle.

23. What is the difference between a language resource and an algorithm?
Answer: A resource supplies information such as examples or lexical entries; an algorithm
specifies how the system processes information.

24. Why is error analysis useful?
Answer: It reveals systematic weaknesses that an overall performance score may conceal
and guides targeted improvements.

25. What is the central challenge of computational linguistics?
Answer: To build useful, testable computational accounts of language while managing
its structure, variability, ambiguity, context, and meaning.

PART B. CHAPTER I — INTRODUCTION (Questions 26–65)
================================================================================

26. What major levels of language may a computational system model?
Answer: Phonetics, phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics, pragmatics, and discourse,
depending on the task.

27. What is phonetics?
Answer: Phonetics studies the physical production, transmission, and perception of speech
sounds.

28. What is phonology?
Answer: Phonology studies how a language organizes sounds into a system of contrasts
and patterns.

29. What is morphology?
Answer: Morphology studies word structure and the ways morphemes form words and word
forms.

30. What is syntax?
Answer: Syntax concerns how words combine into phrases and sentences and the relations
among those units.

31. What is semantics?
Answer: Semantics studies linguistic meaning and the interpretation of words, phrases,
sentences, and relations.

32. What is pragmatics?
Answer: Pragmatics studies how context, speaker intention, and communicative situations
contribute to interpretation.

33. What is discourse analysis?
Answer: It studies relationships across sentences or turns, including reference,
coherence, topic development, and discourse structure.

34. Why do linguistic levels interact?
Answer: Information at one level can constrain or clarify another; for example, syntax
can help resolve word meaning and morphology can signal grammatical relations.

35. What is a token?
Answer: A token is one occurrence of a unit in a sequence, such as a particular
occurrence of a word in a document.

36. What is a type?
Answer: A type is a distinct form counted without counting every repeated occurrence
separately.

37. What is a lemma?
Answer: A lemma is a conventional dictionary form used to group related inflected forms.

38. What is a lexeme?
Answer: A lexeme is an abstract lexical item associated with a family of forms and a
lexical meaning or set of related senses.

39. How do a lemma and a word form differ?
Answer: A lemma is the citation form used to group forms; a word form is the particular
surface form appearing in a text.

40. Why can tokenization be difficult?
Answer: Punctuation, abbreviations, compounds, contractions, URLs, and language-specific
writing conventions complicate the identification of tokens.

41. What is part-of-speech tagging?
Answer: It assigns grammatical categories, such as noun, verb, adjective, or determiner,
to tokens in context.

42. Why is part-of-speech tagging context-sensitive?
Answer: A form can belong to different grammatical categories in different contexts,
such as “book” in “read a book” and “book a room.”

43. What is a syntactic parser?
Answer: A parser assigns a syntactic structure to an input sentence.

44. What is a semantic role?
Answer: A semantic role describes the part a participant plays in an event or relation,
such as agent, patient, recipient, or instrument.

45. What is an agent?
Answer: An agent is typically the participant that initiates or performs an action,
though exact definitions depend on the semantic framework.

46. What is a patient?
Answer: A patient is typically the participant affected by an action or event.

47. Why should grammatical function and semantic role be distinguished?
Answer: Subjects are not always agents, and objects are not always patients; active and
passive sentences can express similar events with different grammatical arrangements.

48. What is a corpus-based method?
Answer: It uses patterns or evidence from collected language data to develop or evaluate
a linguistic analysis or system.

49. What is a rule-based method?
Answer: It relies on explicitly specified rules or constraints to analyze or generate
language.

50. What is a hybrid method?
Answer: It combines approaches, such as learned predictions with symbolic rules or
structured lexical resources.

51. What is a training set?
Answer: It is the data used to fit a model or learn its parameters.

52. What is a development or validation set?
Answer: It is data used to tune a model, select settings, or compare candidate systems
during development.

53. What is a test set?
Answer: It is data reserved for final evaluation to estimate performance on unseen cases.

54. Why should test data be kept separate from training data?
Answer: Separation reduces data leakage and makes evaluation more credible.

55. What is data leakage?
Answer: It occurs when information from evaluation data improperly influences training
or model selection, leading to overly optimistic results.

56. What does representativeness mean in corpus design?
Answer: The data reflects the language varieties, genres, populations, and conditions
relevant to the intended research or deployment.

57. Why might a news corpus be unsuitable for conversational speech research?
Answer: News writing differs from spontaneous conversation in vocabulary, syntax,
disfluency, register, and communicative context.

58. What is inter-annotator agreement?
Answer: It measures the degree to which annotators assign the same labels or structures
to the same data under a shared scheme.

59. Does high inter-annotator agreement prove that an annotation scheme is correct?
Answer: No. Annotators can consistently apply a scheme that fails to capture important
linguistic distinctions.

60. What is descriptive adequacy?
Answer: It concerns how well a linguistic account describes the relevant phenomena.

61. What is computational efficiency?
Answer: It concerns the resources and time required to run a system.

62. Why can a linguistically detailed system be computationally expensive?
Answer: Rich representations and many alternative analyses can increase memory use,
search, and processing time.

63. What is a baseline system?
Answer: A simple reference method used to judge whether a more complex system provides
a meaningful improvement.

64. Why is comparison with a baseline important?
Answer: It shows whether complexity actually improves performance beyond a simple,
transparent alternative.

65. How should a research question guide model design?
Answer: The question defines what information matters, what output is required, and
which representations and evaluation methods are appropriate.

PART C. CHAPTER II — A HISTORICAL OUTLINE (Questions 66–125)
================================================================================

66. Why study the history of computational linguistics?
Answer: Historical approaches introduced concepts such as formal grammars, phrase
structure, transformations, valency, constraints, and meaning-text mapping.

67. What is structuralism in linguistics?
Answer: It emphasizes the organization of linguistic units and their relations within
a language system.

68. What does distributional analysis examine?
Answer: It examines the contexts in which linguistic units occur and how those contexts
help characterize their behavior.

69. What is a formal grammar?
Answer: It is a system of rules or constraints that specifies permitted structures or
sequences.

70. What is a context-free grammar (CFG)?
Answer: A CFG contains production rules in which a nonterminal category can be rewritten
as a sequence of terminals and/or nonterminals.

71. What is a production rule?
Answer: It specifies how one grammatical symbol or category may be expanded into other
symbols or categories.

72. In a rule S → NP VP, what does the rule express?
Answer: It expresses a simplified analysis in which a sentence consists of a noun phrase
followed by a verb phrase.

73. What is a terminal symbol?
Answer: A terminal is a basic symbol of the generated sequence, often a word or token.

74. What is a nonterminal symbol?
Answer: A nonterminal is a grammatical category that can be expanded by grammar rules.

75. What is a derivation?
Answer: A derivation is a sequence of rule applications that produces a string or
structure from a grammar's starting symbol.

76. What is a parse tree?
Answer: It is a tree representation of the hierarchical structure assigned to a sentence.

77. How does a derivation differ from a parse tree?
Answer: A derivation records the steps of rule application; a parse tree displays the
resulting hierarchical organization.

78. What is constituency?
Answer: Constituency groups words into phrases and larger units that function as
structural constituents.

79. What is a constituent?
Answer: A constituent is a unit, such as a noun phrase or verb phrase, that functions
as a component within a larger structure.

80. What is dependency grammar?
Answer: It represents syntactic structure through relations between heads and their
dependents.

81. What is the head of a phrase or dependency?
Answer: The head is the central element that determines important properties or governs
the relation in the chosen analysis.

82. How do constituency and dependency representations differ?
Answer: Constituency emphasizes phrase groupings; dependency emphasizes head-dependent
relations between words.

83. Can a sentence have more than one parse?
Answer: Yes. Structural ambiguity can permit multiple analyses of the same word sequence.

84. What is overgeneration in grammar?
Answer: It occurs when a grammar permits strings or structures that should not be allowed
under the intended account.

85. What is undergeneration?
Answer: It occurs when a grammar fails to permit valid strings or structures.

86. What is generative grammar concerned with?
Answer: It seeks to characterize the structures a finite system of principles can
generate, including novel sentences.

87. Why is a grammar more than a list of memorized sentences?
Answer: Speakers can understand and produce new sentences, so a grammar must capture
general patterns that extend beyond memorized examples.

88. What is a transformation in transformational grammar?
Answer: It is a formal operation or relation used in a theoretical account to connect
structures or explain differences between surface forms.

89. How do active and passive sentences illustrate related structures?
Answer: They can describe similar events while changing which participant is the subject
and how the agent is expressed.

90. Why is word order not always enough to identify semantic roles?
Answer: Different constructions arrange participants differently, and grammatical
subjecthood does not always correspond to the agent role.

91. What is valency?
Answer: Valency describes the complement patterns or participant structures licensed
by a lexical item, especially a predicate.

92. Why is valency lexical information?
Answer: Individual words differ in which complements they require or permit and in the
syntactic forms those complements take.

93. Give an example of a verb with a location complement.
Answer: “Put” commonly requires an object and a location or destination, as in “put the
cup on the table.”

94. Why is “sleep” different from “put” in valency?
Answer: “Sleep” can form a clause without a direct object or location complement, while
the ordinary use of “put” requires additional complement information.

95. What is subcategorization?
Answer: It describes the syntactic categories or complement frames a lexical item
selects or permits.

96. What is a grammatical constraint?
Answer: It is a condition that structures must satisfy to be accepted by a grammar or
model.

97. What is agreement?
Answer: Agreement is a grammatical relationship in which forms share features such as
number, person, or gender where the language requires it.

98. Why can lexical information improve parsing?
Answer: It helps a parser determine which complements and constructions are licensed
by particular words.

99. What is a constraint-based grammar?
Answer: It describes structures through conditions that must be satisfied rather than
only through simple rewrite rules.

100. What is Head-Driven Phrase Structure Grammar (HPSG)?
Answer: HPSG is a constraint-based grammatical framework using structured feature
descriptions and information about heads, complements, and agreement.

101. What is a feature structure?
Answer: It is a representation containing attributes and values, such as category,
number, person, or subcategorization information.

102. What is unification?
Answer: Unification combines compatible feature structures while preserving their
information and rejecting incompatible values.

103. When do feature structures fail to unify?
Answer: They fail when they assign conflicting values to the same feature under the
system's constraints, such as singular versus plural when those values are exclusive.

104. Why is unification useful?
Answer: It allows grammatical information to be combined and checked consistently across
different parts of a structure.

105. Is unification a complete theory of meaning?
Answer: No. It is a formal operation on representations; its capabilities depend on
which features and constraints are encoded.

106. What is Meaning–Text Theory (MTT)?
Answer: It is a linguistic approach that models relationships between meaning and text
through several levels or stages of representation.

107. Why use a multistage meaning-to-text model?
Answer: It separates decisions such as semantic organization, lexical selection,
syntax, morphology, and surface realization.

108. What is a dependency tree?
Answer: It is a tree in which words are linked through directed grammatical dependency
relations.

109. What is a semantic link?
Answer: It is a relation between semantic units, such as an agent relation, cause,
possession, location, or part-whole relation.

110. How does a syntactic dependency differ from a semantic relation?
Answer: A syntactic dependency describes a grammatical relation; a semantic relation
describes a relation in the interpreted content.

111. What is a collocation?
Answer: A collocation is a conventional combination of words, such as “make a decision”
or “strong tea.”

112. Why are collocations important for language generation?
Answer: Grammatically possible alternatives may sound unnatural, so conventional
combinations help produce idiomatic output.

113. What is the relation between grammar and lexicon?
Answer: Grammar describes general structural patterns, while the lexicon records
word-specific information; effective models usually need both.

114. What is the advantage of symbolic linguistic models?
Answer: Their explicit rules and structures can make assumptions and decisions easier
to inspect and explain.

115. What is one limitation of hand-built grammars?
Answer: They can be expensive to construct and may have limited coverage or difficulty
with unexpected variation.

116. How can probabilistic ranking complement a grammar?
Answer: It can rank multiple analyses according to their estimated plausibility in
context.

117. What is a lexical entry?
Answer: It is a record of information about a lexical item, potentially including
category, inflection, meaning, valency, and usage constraints.

118. Why might one word require multiple lexical entries or senses?
Answer: A form can have distinct meanings or grammatical behaviors that need separate
descriptions for accurate analysis.

119. What does a historical comparison of theories reveal?
Answer: It reveals different choices about representation, generalization, lexical
information, structural constraints, and the mapping between form and meaning.

120. Are historical symbolic theories irrelevant to modern systems?
Answer: No. Their representations and distinctions remain useful in grammar design,
error analysis, structured prediction, and hybrid systems.

121. What is the difference between parsing and generation?
Answer: Parsing analyzes an input string into a structure; generation constructs a
string from a representation or communicative goal.

122. Why can a grammar be theoretically adequate but computationally difficult?
Answer: It may allow many possible analyses or require expensive search to find an
appropriate structure.

123. What is a hybrid linguistic model?
Answer: It combines methods, for example a symbolic grammar with a statistical parser
or a neural component constrained by explicit linguistic rules.

124. How can lexical valency help machine translation?
Answer: It can indicate how a source verb's participants are expressed and guide the
choice of a suitable target-language construction.

125. What is the main lesson of the historical chapter?
Answer: Computational descriptions of language need structured representations and
constraints, and different theories emphasize different aspects of that problem.

PART D. CHAPTER III — PRODUCTS OF COMPUTATIONAL LINGUISTICS (Questions 126–195)
================================================================================

126. Why classify language technologies by task?
Answer: Task-based classification clarifies the input, output, linguistic knowledge,
evaluation criteria, and error costs.

127. What is automatic hyphenation?
Answer: It identifies permissible word-division points for line breaks according to
the conventions of a language.

128. Why is hyphenation language-specific?
Answer: Languages differ in syllable patterns, morphology, orthography, and accepted
division conventions.

129. What is spell checking?
Answer: It detects words that may be misspelled and may propose likely corrections.

130. What is edit distance?
Answer: It measures the cost of transforming one string into another through operations
such as insertion, deletion, or substitution.

131. Why can dictionary-only spell checking fail?
Answer: It may miss real-word errors and may wrongly flag valid names, rare words, or
technical terms absent from the dictionary.

132. What is a real-word spelling error?
Answer: It is an error in which the resulting form is a valid word but is incorrect
in the sentence's context.

133. Why is context useful for spell correction?
Answer: It helps distinguish valid words that are plausible in a sentence from valid
words that are likely unintended.

134. What is grammar checking?
Answer: It identifies likely grammatical problems and may suggest corrections.

135. Why is grammar checking difficult?
Answer: Natural language contains exceptions, variation, fragments, quotations, and
context-sensitive constructions that can trigger false alarms.

136. What is precision in grammar checking?
Answer: It is the proportion of flagged problems that are genuine problems under the
chosen standard or annotation policy.

137. What is recall in grammar checking?
Answer: It is the proportion of genuine problems that the checker successfully detects.

138. What is style checking?
Answer: It evaluates writing features such as clarity, concision, consistency, tone,
or repetition rather than only grammatical correctness.

139. Why should style advice be contextual?
Answer: Appropriate style depends on audience, genre, purpose, and register.

140. What is a lexical resource?
Answer: It stores information about words or expressions, such as meanings, forms,
grammatical behavior, and usage patterns.

141. How does a machine lexicon differ from a general dictionary?
Answer: A machine lexicon typically stores structured fields for computational access,
whereas a general dictionary prioritizes human-readable explanations.

142. What is information retrieval?
Answer: It finds and ranks documents or passages that may be relevant to a query.

143. How does information retrieval differ from question answering?
Answer: Retrieval returns potentially useful material; question answering aims to
formulate a response based on evidence or interpretation.

144. What is ranking in a search system?
Answer: Ranking orders candidate results by estimated relevance or usefulness.

145. Why can a search query be ambiguous?
Answer: A term may have several meanings or refer to different entities or domains.

146. What is query expansion?
Answer: It adds related terms or representations to a query to improve retrieval.

147. What is precision at k?
Answer: It measures the proportion of the top k retrieved results that are relevant.

148. What is recall in information retrieval?
Answer: It measures the proportion of all relevant items that the system retrieves.

149. What is automatic summarization?
Answer: It creates a shorter text intended to preserve important information from a source.

150. What is extractive summarization?
Answer: It selects existing sentences or passages from the source.

151. What is abstractive summarization?
Answer: It generates new wording to express the source's main information.

152. Why is summarization not simply deletion?
Answer: A good summary must preserve central claims, important relations, and necessary
qualifications while removing less important material.

153. What is factuality in summarization?
Answer: Factuality means that the summary's claims are supported by the source and do
not introduce invented or distorted information.

154. Why can overlap metrics be insufficient for summaries?
Answer: They may penalize valid paraphrases or reward word overlap without checking
whether the main meaning is accurately preserved.

155. What is machine translation?
Answer: It automatically converts text from a source language into a target language.

156. Why is machine translation more than word replacement?
Answer: Languages differ in syntax, morphology, idioms, lexical distinctions, and how
they express information and relations.

157. What is translation adequacy?
Answer: It concerns how well a translation preserves the relevant content and meaning
of the source.

158. What is translation fluency?
Answer: It concerns how natural and well-formed the target-language output is.

159. Can a translation be fluent but inaccurate?
Answer: Yes. Natural-sounding text may omit, add, or distort source information.

160. What is a natural-language interface?
Answer: It lets users communicate with a database, application, or service using
ordinary language.

161. What is intent detection?
Answer: It identifies the action or communicative goal a user expresses, such as asking
for information or requesting a change.

162. Why should a natural-language interface ask clarifying questions?
Answer: Clarification helps resolve missing or ambiguous details before a potentially
incorrect action is taken.

163. Why is understanding a command different from being authorized to execute it?
Answer: Interpretation identifies what the user requests; authorization checks whether
the action is permitted and safe.

164. What is information extraction?
Answer: It converts information in unstructured text into structured fields or records.

165. What is named-entity recognition?
Answer: It identifies spans that refer to entities such as people, organizations,
locations, dates, or other predefined types.

166. What is relation extraction?
Answer: It identifies relations between entities or events described in text.

167. What is event extraction?
Answer: It identifies events and relevant details such as participants, time, place,
status, and relations.

168. Why must extraction systems detect negation?
Answer: Without negation handling, “The company did not acquire the firm” could be
incorrectly recorded as a positive acquisition.

169. What is coreference resolution?
Answer: It identifies expressions that refer to the same entity, such as a name and a
later pronoun.

170. What is entity normalization?
Answer: It maps different mentions or formats to a standard representation when the
evidence supports doing so.

171. What is text generation?
Answer: It produces natural-language text from structured data, a meaning
representation, a goal, or other input.

172. What is surface realization?
Answer: It converts a selected content and structure into an actual linguistic form,
including word forms and ordering.

173. Why must generated text be checked for factual accuracy?
Answer: A system may produce grammatical language that changes dates, quantities,
participants, or other facts.

174. What is language understanding?
Answer: It is a broad term for inferring relevant linguistic, semantic, or communicative
information from language input.

175. Does successful intent classification prove full language understanding?
Answer: No. A system may identify a broad task without resolving reference, factual
content, implications, or discourse context.

176. Why is speech recognition related to computational linguistics?
Answer: It maps speech signals to linguistic units and may use lexical, phonological,
syntactic, and contextual information.

177. Why is speech synthesis related to language generation?
Answer: It converts linguistic content into speech, requiring choices about pronunciation,
prosody, rhythm, and timing.

178. What is an end-to-end language application?
Answer: It is the complete user-facing system, including its connected components,
rather than one isolated processing module.

179. Why can component-level scores be misleading?
Answer: A component can perform well alone but fail to improve the complete application
because its errors or delays affect downstream steps.

180. What is domain shift?
Answer: It occurs when deployment data differs substantially from the data used for
training or development.

181. How can domain shift affect spell checking?
Answer: A checker trained on general language may flag specialist terms or new vocabulary
from a technical domain.

182. How can domain shift affect machine translation?
Answer: A system may mistranslate domain-specific terminology or fail on sentence
patterns uncommon in its training data.

183. Why are high-stakes applications different?
Answer: Errors may have serious consequences, so stronger validation, transparency,
human review, and uncertainty handling may be needed.

184. What is a false alarm in style checking?
Answer: It is a suggestion that incorrectly treats an acceptable stylistic choice as
a problem.

185. Why should evaluation include realistic user data?
Answer: Realistic data reveals variation and errors that carefully selected examples
may hide.

186. What is human-in-the-loop processing?
Answer: It includes a human reviewer or decision-maker in the system's workflow.

187. Why might human review be useful in information extraction?
Answer: It can verify uncertain or consequential facts before they are stored or acted on.

188. What is a knowledge base?
Answer: It is a structured collection of facts, entities, relations, or rules that a
system can consult.

189. How can a knowledge base support question answering?
Answer: It can provide structured facts or relations that help interpret a question
and construct an evidence-based answer.

190. What is terminology management?
Answer: It records and controls domain-specific terms and their preferred forms,
especially across documents or translations.

191. Why is consistency important in technical translation?
Answer: Different translations of the same term can confuse readers or change the
intended technical distinction.

192. What is a user-centered evaluation?
Answer: It assesses whether a system helps users accomplish their actual goals, not
only whether it scores well on an abstract metric.

193. Why should language tools explain suggestions?
Answer: Explanations help users judge whether a recommendation applies and learn from
the system's feedback.

194. What common principle links spell checking, translation, and summarization?
Answer: Each requires a defined task, suitable resources, context-sensitive decisions,
and evaluation of errors that matter to users.

195. What is the main lesson of the applications chapter?
Answer: Language technologies share core linguistic challenges, but each task needs
its own representations, resources, evaluation, and error policy.

PART E. CHAPTER IV — LANGUAGE AS A MEANING ⇔ TEXT TRANSFORMER (Questions 196–250)
================================================================================

196. What does it mean to view language as a meaning-text transformer?
Answer: It models relationships between conceptual content and linguistic expressions,
in both interpretation and production.

197. What are the two broad directions of a meaning-text model?
Answer: Meaning to text during production, and text to meaning during interpretation.

198. Are meaning-to-text and text-to-meaning exact inverses?
Answer: No. Different texts can express similar meanings, and one text can have multiple
interpretations.

199. Why can one meaning be expressed by different sentences?
Answer: Speakers can choose synonyms, paraphrases, different syntactic constructions,
or different levels of detail.

200. What is synonymy?
Answer: It is a relation between expressions with similar or overlapping meanings.

201. Are synonyms always interchangeable?
Answer: No. They can differ in register, connotation, collocation, grammar, or emphasis.

202. What is homonymy?
Answer: It involves a single form associated with distinct meanings, such as “bank”
for a financial institution and a river edge.

203. What is lexical ambiguity?
Answer: It occurs when a word or lexical form has more than one possible meaning or
grammatical interpretation.

204. What is syntactic ambiguity?
Answer: It occurs when a sequence of words permits more than one syntactic structure.

205. What is referential ambiguity?
Answer: It occurs when an expression, often a pronoun or description, could refer to
more than one entity.

206. What is pragmatic ambiguity?
Answer: It occurs when the intended communicative force or implication is uncertain
from the wording and available context.

207. What is attachment ambiguity?
Answer: It occurs when a phrase can be structurally associated with different parts
of a sentence.

208. Give an example of attachment ambiguity.
Answer: “I photographed the actor with the camera” may mean the camera was used to
take the photograph or that the actor had the camera.

209. What is scope ambiguity?
Answer: It occurs when the relative scope of expressions such as quantifiers permits
different interpretations.

210. How can context resolve lexical ambiguity?
Answer: Nearby words, topic, discourse, and world knowledge can make one sense more
plausible than alternatives.

211. Does context always eliminate ambiguity?
Answer: No. Context may be insufficient, contradictory, or compatible with more than
one interpretation.

212. What is semantic representation?
Answer: It is a formal or structured account of selected meaning information needed
for an analysis or task.

213. Why can a semantic representation be task-specific?
Answer: Different applications require different information; a search system and an
event database do not need identical representations.

214. What is a predicate in a semantic representation?
Answer: It expresses a property, relation, or event that can connect to participants
or arguments.

215. What is an event representation?
Answer: It represents an event and may specify its type, participants, time, location,
instrument, or other attributes.

216. What is a semantic network?
Answer: It represents concepts or entities as nodes connected by labeled relations.

217. How does a semantic graph differ from a syntactic tree?
Answer: A syntactic tree represents grammatical organization; a semantic graph represents
meaning relations and may contain links not captured by syntax alone.

218. What is compositionality?
Answer: It is the principle that the meaning of a complex expression is determined in
part by the meanings of its components and how they are combined.

219. Why does compositionality matter for parsing?
Answer: A system needs to combine the meanings of words and phrases according to
structure rather than interpret every sentence as an isolated whole.

220. What is a non-compositional expression?
Answer: It is an expression whose conventional meaning cannot be fully predicted by
combining the ordinary meanings of its parts, as with many idioms.

221. Why is semantic decomposition useful?
Answer: It breaks complex content into smaller components that can be represented,
compared, reused, or reasoned about.

222. What is a semantic primitive?
Answer: It is a basic element in a chosen semantic representation from which more
complex descriptions may be built.

223. What is a risk of using too few semantic primitives?
Answer: The representation may fail to distinguish meanings that matter for the task.

224. What is a risk of using too many semantic primitives?
Answer: The representation may become difficult to maintain, learn, and generalize.

225. What is the difference between sentence meaning and speaker intention?
Answer: Sentence meaning concerns conventional linguistic interpretation; speaker
intention concerns what the speaker is trying to communicate in context.

226. What is an implicature?
Answer: It is an inferred meaning that is not directly stated but is suggested by the
utterance and context.

227. Why can a literal interpretation be insufficient?
Answer: Speakers use idioms, indirect requests, irony, metaphor, and context-dependent
expressions that go beyond literal word meanings.

228. What is coreference in discourse?
Answer: It is the relation in which multiple expressions refer to the same entity.

229. Why is discourse context important for pronoun interpretation?
Answer: The intended referent may have been introduced in an earlier sentence or turn.

230. What is an antecedent?
Answer: It is an earlier expression or discourse entity to which a later expression,
such as a pronoun, refers.

231. Why is text segmentation important?
Answer: Systems need to identify meaningful units such as tokens, sentences, paragraphs,
and sections before applying later analysis.

232. Why can punctuation be an unreliable sentence-boundary clue?
Answer: Periods can occur in abbreviations, decimals, initials, URLs, and other contexts
that do not mark sentence endings.

233. What is a multistage language-processing model?
Answer: It separates processing into stages such as lexical analysis, syntax, semantics,
and discourse interpretation.

234. What is one advantage of modular processing?
Answer: Components can be developed, evaluated, and diagnosed separately.

235. What is one disadvantage of a strict pipeline?
Answer: Early errors may propagate, and useful alternative analyses may be discarded
before later evidence becomes available.

236. How can a system preserve uncertainty?
Answer: It can retain multiple candidate analyses, attach confidence estimates, or
request clarification rather than forcing a premature choice.

237. What is machine translation in a meaning-text perspective?
Answer: It transforms source text through interpretation or intermediate representation
into target-language text.

238. Why can languages package the same content differently?
Answer: They differ in word order, morphology, grammatical categories, idioms, and
conventions for expressing relations or information structure.

239. What is lexicalization?
Answer: It is the selection of lexical items to express intended content.

240. What is morphological realization?
Answer: It chooses appropriate word forms, such as tense, number, case, or agreement.

241. What is syntactic realization?
Answer: It organizes selected words and phrases into a grammatical structure.

242. Why are idioms difficult for literal translation?
Answer: Their conventional meanings may not follow from the literal meanings of their
individual words.

243. What is adequacy in translation?
Answer: It concerns preservation of the source's relevant meaning and information.

244. What is fluency in translation?
Answer: It concerns whether the target text reads naturally and follows target-language
conventions.

245. Can two different translations both be acceptable?
Answer: Yes. They may preserve the same important content while differing in wording,
structure, tone, or emphasis.

246. Why should translation preserve uncertainty?
Answer: The source may be ambiguous or underspecified; a translation should not invent
a definite interpretation without sufficient evidence.

247. What is a paraphrase?
Answer: It is an alternative expression of substantially similar content.

248. How can paraphrase recognition support information retrieval?
Answer: It helps identify documents or passages that express a query's idea using
different words.

249. What is the main lesson of the meaning-text chapter?
Answer: Language maps between meaning and expression in complex, non-unique ways, and
context and intermediate representations help manage that complexity.

250. Why is meaning representation central to generation and understanding?
Answer: It provides a structured bridge between linguistic form and the content a
system is intended to interpret or express.

PART F. CHAPTER V — LINGUISTIC MODELS (Questions 251–300)
================================================================================

251. What is a linguistic model?
Answer: It is an explicit account of selected linguistic properties designed to explain
phenomena or support a computational task.

252. Is a model identical to language itself?
Answer: No. A model simplifies and represents selected aspects of language for a
specific purpose.

253. What is descriptive adequacy in model design?
Answer: It is the extent to which the model captures the relevant linguistic patterns.

254. What is a procedural model?
Answer: It specifies how a system performs operations to analyze, search for, or generate
structures.

255. How does a descriptive model differ from a procedural model?
Answer: A descriptive model specifies what structures or relations are possible; a
procedural model specifies how a computation obtains or uses them.

256. Why does a formal grammar not automatically provide an efficient parser?
Answer: The grammar may allow many analyses, and the parser still needs an algorithm
to search for the appropriate structure.

257. What is a symbolic model?
Answer: It represents knowledge through explicit symbols, rules, categories, and
constraints.

258. What is a statistical model?
Answer: It estimates patterns or probabilities from data and uses them to make predictions
or rank alternatives.

259. What is a neural model?
Answer: It uses parameterized neural networks to learn representations and mappings
from data.

260. What is one strength of symbolic models?
Answer: Their explicit rules and structures can make assumptions easier to inspect.

261. What is one limitation of symbolic models?
Answer: Hand-built rules and resources can be expensive to create and may not cover
unexpected language variation.

262. What is one strength of statistical or neural models?
Answer: They can learn complex patterns from data and generalize beyond individually
written rules when training data is suitable.

263. What is one limitation of learned models?
Answer: They can inherit data biases, fail under domain shift, and produce outputs that
are difficult to explain or verify.

264. What is a hybrid model?
Answer: It combines symbolic, statistical, neural, or resource-based components.

265. What is overfitting?
Answer: It occurs when a model fits training data too closely and performs poorly on
new relevant data.

266. How can a researcher detect overfitting?
Answer: Compare training and held-out performance, inspect learning curves, and test
on properly separated data.

267. What is underfitting?
Answer: It occurs when a model is too limited to capture important patterns even in
the training data.

268. What is domain adaptation?
Answer: It is the process of improving a model's performance for a target domain that
differs from its original training domain.

269. What is a feature in a model?
Answer: It is a represented property or signal used in prediction, such as word form,
context, grammatical category, or a learned numerical representation.

270. What is a parameter?
Answer: It is a value learned or set within a model that affects its behavior.

271. What is a hyperparameter?
Answer: It is a configuration choice set outside the ordinary parameter-learning
process, such as model size or a regularization strength.

272. Why should training, development, and test sets be separate?
Answer: They serve different roles: fitting, tuning, and final evaluation; separation
reduces optimistic bias.

273. What is cross-validation?
Answer: It evaluates a method across multiple train-validation splits to estimate
how performance varies across data partitions.

274. What is a baseline?
Answer: It is a simple comparison system used to determine whether a more complex method
offers a meaningful improvement.

275. Why is interpretability important?
Answer: It helps researchers or users understand why a system made a decision and
identify possible errors or inappropriate assumptions.

276. Does interpretability guarantee correctness?
Answer: No. An easily understood rule or explanation can still be wrong or incomplete.

277. What is model calibration?
Answer: Calibration concerns whether confidence estimates correspond to observed
frequencies of correctness.

278. Why is uncertainty estimation useful?
Answer: It can help systems defer, request clarification, or trigger human review when
the evidence is weak.

279. What is abstention in a predictive system?
Answer: It is the choice not to make a definitive prediction when confidence or evidence
is insufficient.

280. What is a linguistic resource?
Answer: It is a structured source of language information, such as a lexicon, corpus,
grammar, ontology, or annotated dataset.

281. Why is resource documentation important?
Answer: It explains how the resource was created, what it covers, how labels are defined,
and where it may be unreliable.

282. What is annotation bias?
Answer: It occurs when annotation decisions systematically reflect inconsistent
guidelines, limited perspectives, or assumptions that affect labels.

283. Why does data representativeness matter for fairness?
Answer: Underrepresented varieties or groups may receive worse performance if the
system is evaluated and trained mainly on other populations.

284. What is reproducibility?
Answer: It is the ability to understand and, where possible, repeat an experiment using
documented data, procedures, and configurations.

285. Why should researchers report limitations?
Answer: Limitations help readers interpret results and avoid applying conclusions
outside the evidence or intended scope.

286. What is an ablation study?
Answer: It tests the contribution of a component by removing or changing it and measuring
the effect on performance.

287. Why is ablation useful?
Answer: It can reveal whether a component contributes real value or merely adds
complexity without measurable benefit.

288. What is error analysis?
Answer: It is the systematic examination of incorrect outputs to identify recurring
causes and opportunities for improvement.

289. What is a confusion matrix?
Answer: It is a table comparing predicted classes with reference classes, showing
correct and incorrect classifications by category.

290. When can accuracy be misleading?
Answer: It can conceal poor performance on rare classes or cases where false positives
and false negatives have very different costs.

291. What is the F1 score?
Answer: It is the harmonic mean of precision and recall.

292. Why should metrics match the task?
Answer: A metric should measure the outcome that matters; token accuracy, factuality,
ranking quality, and user success are different objectives.

293. What is data shift?
Answer: It is a change between the data distribution used for development and the
distribution encountered in evaluation or deployment.

294. What is responsible language technology?
Answer: It includes careful evaluation, privacy protection, bias analysis, transparent
limitations, and safeguards appropriate to the application's risks.

295. Why can dialect variation be misclassified as error?
Answer: A system trained on one standard or variety may treat valid forms from another
variety as incorrect because its data and rules are too narrow.

296. What is a high-stakes language application?
Answer: It is an application where errors can have substantial consequences, such as
some medical, legal, financial, or public-service uses.

297. Why may high-stakes applications require human review?
Answer: Review can catch consequential mistakes and assess context or evidence the
automated system may miss.

298. How should one choose between symbolic and neural methods?
Answer: Compare task needs, data availability, coverage, interpretability, latency,
maintenance, and empirical results rather than assuming one method is always best.

299. Why should models be tested on adversarial or difficult examples?
Answer: Such examples can expose brittle shortcuts and weaknesses hidden by ordinary
test data.

300. What is the main lesson of the linguistic-models chapter?
Answer: Models are purposeful simplifications that need appropriate resources,
evaluation, uncertainty handling, and continual examination of their errors.

PART G. INTEGRATED APPLICATION AND ANALYSIS QUESTIONS (301–340)
================================================================================

301. A system flags “their” in “Their going to arrive.” What issue is it detecting?
Answer: It is likely detecting a real-word grammatical or contextual error, where
“they're” may be intended.

302. Why might a spell checker wrongly flag a person's surname?
Answer: The surname may be absent from the lexicon even though it is valid.

303. A parser gives two structures for one sentence. Is that necessarily a failure?
Answer: No. It may correctly represent genuine structural ambiguity; a later component
can rank or resolve the alternatives.

304. Why might a summary omit a key qualification?
Answer: The summarizer may prioritize prominent content without adequately representing
the qualification or its relation to the main claim.

305. A translation changes “may” to “will.” What kind of problem is this?
Answer: It changes modality and certainty, potentially making the target text stronger
than the source.

306. Why should an extraction system distinguish a proposed event from a completed event?
Answer: Otherwise it may store a plan or possibility as an established fact.

307. A question-answering system retrieves a relevant document but answers incorrectly. Where might the error occur?
Answer: It may occur during passage selection, interpretation, reasoning, answer
generation, or failure to verify evidence.

308. Why should a text generator use structured input data?
Answer: Structured data can help preserve exact facts and keep content selection
separate from linguistic realization.

309. A grammar checker flags many dialect forms. What should developers investigate?
Answer: Training data, annotation policy, the target language standard, evaluation
coverage, and the distinction between variation and error.

310. A neural classifier works on news but fails on social media. What is a likely explanation?
Answer: Domain shift, including different vocabulary, spelling, syntax, and communicative
conventions.

311. Why should a language system preserve source evidence?
Answer: Evidence allows users and reviewers to verify claims and diagnose unsupported
outputs.

312. How can a system reduce false positives?
Answer: Improve context modeling, resources, decision thresholds, annotation quality,
and evaluation on legitimate edge cases.

313. How can a system reduce false negatives?
Answer: Increase relevant coverage, improve detection signals, adjust thresholds, and
analyze missed cases.

314. Why is a confidence score not automatically trustworthy?
Answer: The model may be poorly calibrated or overconfident on unfamiliar inputs.

315. Why should a search engine return diverse results for ambiguous queries?
Answer: Diversity can expose several plausible interpretations rather than prematurely
committing to one.

316. What is the role of lexical resources in translation?
Answer: They provide senses, inflection, valency, terminology, and collocation information
that can guide lexical and structural choices.

317. Why might a literal translation be misleading even when its words are correct?
Answer: Idioms, grammatical conventions, and contextual meaning may not align word by
word across languages.

318. How can dependency parsing support information extraction?
Answer: It can identify grammatical relations that help connect predicates to entities
and distinguish participants.

319. Why is dependency parsing not sufficient for all event extraction?
Answer: Event extraction may also require coreference, semantic interpretation, negation,
modality, temporal reasoning, and document context.

320. How does discourse context help coreference resolution?
Answer: It provides earlier entities, topic continuity, grammatical cues, and semantic
compatibility that help identify a pronoun's referent.

321. Why can a valid sentence be difficult for a simple grammar?
Answer: It may contain an unmodeled construction, long-distance dependency, variation,
or a lexical pattern missing from the grammar.

322. What is the danger of evaluating only average performance?
Answer: It can hide systematic failures on particular languages, genres, groups, or
linguistic constructions.

323. Why is a held-out test set important for model comparison?
Answer: It provides a common, less biased basis for estimating generalization, provided
the test set is not repeatedly used for tuning.

324. What is the difference between a model error and a data error?
Answer: A model error arises from the system's analysis or prediction; a data error may
come from incorrect, incomplete, or inconsistent input or reference annotation.

325. Why should annotation disagreements be reviewed?
Answer: They can reveal ambiguous examples, unclear guidelines, or weaknesses in the
annotation scheme.

326. How could a natural-language interface handle an ambiguous date?
Answer: It can use context and locale conventions or ask the user to clarify before
performing an important action.

327. Why is authorization separate from semantic interpretation?
Answer: A system can understand an instruction without having permission to carry it out.

328. How can collocation knowledge improve generated text?
Answer: It encourages conventional word combinations and reduces awkward but technically
grammatical expressions.

329. What is the difference between grammaticality and truth?
Answer: Grammaticality concerns structural well-formedness; truth concerns whether a
proposition is accurate in a particular situation or model.

330. What is the difference between meaning and plausibility?
Answer: Meaning concerns interpretation; plausibility concerns how likely or realistic
that interpretation seems given world knowledge and context.

331. Why can an unusual sentence still be grammatically structured?
Answer: Its syntax may follow grammatical patterns even if its content is implausible,
metaphorical, or contradictory.

332. Why is the choice of semantic primitives theoretically important?
Answer: It determines which distinctions a system can express and which inferences it
can support.

333. What does it mean to preserve uncertainty in translation?
Answer: The translation should avoid resolving ambiguity more strongly than the source
or evidence permits.

334. How can a hybrid system combine a neural model with a grammar?
Answer: The neural model can propose or rank analyses while grammatical constraints
check structure or restrict invalid outputs.

335. Why should a simple baseline be included in an experiment?
Answer: It shows whether the proposed method improves on an inexpensive reference and
helps justify added complexity.

336. What should be documented for a reproducible experiment?
Answer: Data selection, preprocessing, annotation, splits, model settings, evaluation
metrics, software versions, and relevant implementation details.

337. How can privacy constrain corpus sharing?
Answer: Personal or sensitive content may require consent, anonymization, restricted
access, or non-public release.

338. Why is system maintenance part of language technology?
Answer: Language changes, new terminology appears, domains shift, and errors or security
issues require updates.

339. What is the value of a human-centered evaluation?
Answer: It tests whether the system actually helps users accomplish their goals and
whether its errors are acceptable in real use.

340. What unifies the major themes of the book?
Answer: The need to represent language explicitly, manage the relationship between form
and meaning, use suitable resources, build workable models, and evaluate real performance.

341. Define morphology.

342. Define syntax.

343. Define semantics.
Answer: The study of linguistic meaning.

344. Define pragmatics.
Answer: The study of context-dependent interpretation and communicative use.

345. Define corpus.
Answer: A collection of language data assembled for a defined purpose.

346. Define lexicon.
Answer: A resource describing lexical items and their properties.

347. Define parser.
Answer: A system that assigns a structural analysis to linguistic input.

348. Define generator.
Answer: A system that produces linguistic output from a representation or goal.

349. Define ambiguity.
Answer: The possibility of multiple analyses or interpretations.

350. Define valency.
Answer: The complement or participant patterns associated with a lexical item.

351. Define unification.
Answer: The combination of compatible feature structures under a constraint system.

352. Define collocation.
Answer: A conventional or recurrent combination of words.

353. Define information retrieval.
Answer: The process of finding and ranking relevant documents or passages.

354. Define information extraction.
Answer: The conversion of information in text into structured records.

355. Define summarization.
Answer: The production of a shorter representation that preserves important source
information.

356. Define machine translation.
Answer: The automatic conversion of text from one language to another.

357. Define precision.
Answer: The proportion of predicted positive cases that are correct.

358. Define recall.
Answer: The proportion of relevant positive cases that are found.

359. Define overfitting.
Answer: Excessive adaptation to training data that harms generalization.

360. Define domain shift.
Answer: A difference between development data and the data encountered in use.

361. Define coreference.
Answer: The relation in which two or more expressions refer to the same entity.

362. Define named-entity recognition.

linguistic form.

365. Define reproducibility.
Answer: The ability to understand and, where possible, repeat a study from its documented
data and procedures.
